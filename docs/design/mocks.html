Each route validates its search params with Zod validateSearch: bad params fall back to defaults. Copy never hard-codes a count ({rules.length} rows), and one test asserts that the strip, facets and badges agree at the frozen clock. That stops the “17 rows” class of error.
4 · “Approve 3” → mutation and invalidation
  1. POST /inbox/items:approve {ids:[4821,4836,4840]}. The server re-checks state and tier and returns 202 {runId, counts}.
  2. Optimistic: remove the 3 items from ['inbox'] and move the selection to the next item (URL replace). Snapshot first.
  3. onSuccess: write counts into the cache so the badges move at once, then invalidate inbox, claims, activity and settings/preview.
  4. onError: roll back the snapshot. A per-item 409 restores only that item, with its reason.
  5. Not optimistic: the run itself. Claim statuses change only when step.completed arrives.
5 · Agent progress · SSE
  1. GET /runs/{id}/events: step.started | step.completed | step.failed | run.completed, each with an id.
  2. Reconnect sends Last-Event-ID, and the server replays from the RunStep log.
  3. Tab reload: the route loader refetches run (snapshot + lastEventId), then resubscribes from there.
  4. A RunsProvider at the app root holds active runs, so “5 agents active” stays live after you leave the screen.
  5. A failed run makes a new InboxItem on the server. There is no client retry: retrying is a new approval.
  6. If a proxy blocks SSE, fall back to polling GET /runs/{id} every 2 s.
6 · Hold-window race · #4859

7 · Tables at scale · Claims at a DSO (4,000 rows)

8 · Role gating

What this bets on

The coordinator’s job is exceptions, so the product is the queue of agent proposals; everything else is reference. It bets that a fast, evidence-backed approve loop is what builds trust in autonomous agents.

Pros

  • Clearest answer to “what does the human do?” — the approve loop is the whole demo
  • Evidence, confidence and undo sit next to every decision
  • One item shape scales to every flow (eligibility, appeals, payments)
  • Cheap to build well: list + detail + fake async progress

Cons

  • No spatial sense of where money is stuck; A/R health is invisible from home
  • Email/Linear metaphor is generic — nothing says “dental” or “revenue cycle”
  • Queue can feel like a chore list; a quiet day is an empty screen
  • Claim and patient context are secondary, so browsing is weaker

Best for

A one-person billing office or a pitch about human-in-the-loop trust: show the agent working and the human saying yes.

What this bets on

The expensive part of patient billing is the conversation: “why do I owe this?”, “can I split it?”, “I already paid”. An agent answers by text and email, quoting the EOB line, offers plans inside rules Dr. Morgan signed, and sends reminders. Anything clinical, any dispute and any waiver goes to a person. It bets on the agentic promise on the patient side.

Pros

  • Answers the question that stops payment, at 8 PM, with the EOB line
  • Plans inside signed rules close without Dana (Park: 0 touches in 5 weeks)
  • Clear boundaries: clinical, disputes and waivers always reach a person
  • Fits patients who never open paper statements

Cons

  • Highest risk: a wrong or tone-deaf message reaches a patient directly
  • Needs consent, quiet hours and opt-out handling done right (TCPA)
  • Patients may want a person; the escalation path must be fast
  • Statements still have to exist under it; this adds a channel, not a replacement

Best for

A practice with a large self-pay or high-deductible share, and a pitch about what agents can do beyond claims. Pairs with A or B underneath.
