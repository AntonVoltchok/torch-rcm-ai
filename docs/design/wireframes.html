Dental RCM: how the money flows, and the screens that move it

Low-fidelity wireframes · sample data dated Wed Oct 28, 2026 (“today”) · fake patients, generic payer names

How money flows through a dental practice. A patient’s dental insurance (the payer: Delta Dental, MetLife, Cigna…) pays for part of most visits, but only after the practice proves the work was covered, coded correctly and documented. Before the visit, someone checks what the plan will cover. After the visit, the procedures recorded in the practice management system (PMS) become a claim, which goes through a clearinghouse to the payer. Then the practice waits, often 30–90+ days, chasing the payer until the claim is paid, denied or partly paid. The payment comes back with an explanation of benefits (EOB; ERA when electronic), gets posted against the claim, and whatever insurance didn’t cover becomes a patient statement. Until the money lands, it sits in accounts receivable (A/R). The older it gets, the less likely it is ever collected.

What this product does. AI agents do the tedious back-and-forth (portal lookups, phone calls to payers, pulling x-rays, drafting appeals, matching payments). One human, the practice’s billing coordinator, supervises: she approves, edits or rejects what the agents propose and takes the exceptions they can’t resolve. The UI is built around that review loop: agent proposes → human decides → agent executes → audit trail.

Who uses it

  • Billing coordinator (“Dana S.”). Lives in §1 all day and drills into §2–§7.
  • Front desk. Receives eligibility flags from §2 and talks money with patients before the visit.
  • Practice owner / dentist (“Dr. Morgan”). Checks §8 weekly: is cash coming in, and is it slowing down?
  • Agents. Not users, but actors: every action they take shows up in timelines with a dashed ✦ marker.

Ten-second glossary

Payer
the insurance company
PMS
practice management system: schedule, chart and ledger; source of truth for procedures
CDT code
procedure code, e.g. D2740 = porcelain crown
Clearinghouse
middleman that validates and routes claims to payers
Rejection vs denial

practice’s price / payer’s contracted price / what the payer actually sent

contractual write-off: fee minus allowed. The practice never collects it
Patient resp. (PR)
deductible / coinsurance the patient owes
Annual max
cap on what the plan pays per year (often $1,000–$2,000)
Frequency limit
“2 cleanings/yr”, “crown once per 5 yrs per tooth”
Downgrade
payer pays for a cheaper alternative (composite paid as amalgam)
COB
coordination of benefits: which plan pays first when there are two
A/R
accounts receivable: money billed but not yet collected

1Home: “Needs your attention” queue

Billing coordinatorevery morning, then all day

Today a billing coordinator starts her day across six payer portals, a PMS, a fax inbox and a stack of sticky notes, deciding by gut what to chase first. Here the agents have already worked overnight. Everything they could finish is done, and everything that needs a human decision lands in one queue ranked by dollars at stake and urgency. Each item is a proposal: what happened, what the agent wants to do, the evidence, and how confident it is. She approves, edits, rejects or snoozes it, and the agent carries out the decision.

Item types
denial fix · eligibility flag · payment mismatch · follow-up escalation · appeal ready · patient question
Sort order
priority (deadline × $) then age
Design rule
every agent action must be undoable or at least auditable

2Eligibility & benefits verification

Front desk + billing coordinator1–3 days before each visit

Before a patient sits in the chair, someone has to confirm the insurance is active and work out what it will actually pay: how much of the annual maximum is left, whether the deductible is met, whether this cleaning or x-ray is allowed yet (frequency limits), and whether a waiting period or missing-tooth clause applies. Today staff spend 10–20 minutes per patient on portals and phone hold. Skipping it is how surprise bills and denials happen. The agent checks every appointment on the schedule automatically and surfaces only the problems: coverage gaps, pre-auth needed, or scheduled work that exceeds remaining benefits.

Data source
payer eligibility response, portal scrape or IVR call; plus PMS history for “last done” dates
Key output
insurance estimate vs patient estimate per appointment
Hand-off
flag to front desk to talk cost with the patient before the visit

3Claim creation & submission

Billing coordinatorsame day or day after the visit

When the dentist finishes a visit, the procedures are already in the PMS as CDT codes with tooth numbers and surfaces. Turning them into a claim that won’t bounce is the hard part. Many procedures need attachments (x-rays, perio chart, a written narrative, pre-auth number), and a missing one is the single most common reason a claim gets denied weeks later. The agent builds the draft claim, auto-pulls the attachments it can find in the PMS and imaging system, drafts the narrative from chart notes, runs “scrubber” checks, and asks the human only about what’s missing.

Line item
CDT code · tooth (1–32) · surface (M,O,D,B,L…) or quadrant (UR/UL/LR/LL) · fee
“Clean claim”
accepted and paid on first pass, with no rework
Submission result
clearinghouse accepts or rejects (format/data), then the payer acknowledges

4Claims pipeline & follow-up

Billing coordinatorweekly sweeps; daily for anything 60+ days

After submission, claims sit “pending” for 30–90+ days with no news. Payers don’t proactively say “we lost your x-ray.” Someone has to check each claim’s status on a portal or sit on hold with the payer’s phone line (IVR), then resend paperwork. Claims nobody chases age past 90 days and often become uncollectable. Agents do the routine status checks and calls on a schedule, log every touch on the claim’s timeline, and escalate to the human only when they’re stuck.

5Denials & appeals

Billing coordinator (+ dentist for clinical sign-off)within days of the denial; appeal deadlines ~180 days

A denial arrives as a cryptic code on an EOB (CO-197 pre-auth absent, CO-119 benefit max or frequency reached, CO-204 not covered). Someone has to decode it, find the real root cause (our mistake? payer mistake? plan rule?), decide whether to correct and resubmit, appeal, or bill the patient, and then write an appeal letter with evidence. Many practices simply don’t appeal, and that money is lost. The agent translates the code, investigates the root cause across PMS and payer history, drafts the appeal with exhibits, and tracks patterns so the practice stops repeating the same denial.

Common reasons
missing attachment · frequency · not covered · needs narrative · duplicate · COB · pre-auth required · downgrade
Three outcomes
correct & resubmit · appeal · accept & bill patient / write off

6Payment posting

Billing coordinatordaily, as payments arrive

Payers send money (EFT/check) plus a remittance that explains it: an ERA (electronic file) or a paper/PDF EOB. One payment often covers many claims and patients. Each line has to be matched to the right claim and procedure and posted into the PMS ledger: what was billed, what the payer “allowed” under contract, what it paid, the contractual write-off, and what the patient owes. Done by hand, it’s slow, error-prone keying, and underpayments slip through unnoticed. The agent auto-matches and posts the clean lines and holds back only mismatches and possible underpayments for review.

Per line
billed = paid + adjustment + patient responsibility
Adjustment codes
CO-45 contractual write-off · PR-1 deductible · PR-2 coinsurance
Also
reconcile the payment total to the bank deposit

7Patient billing

Billing coordinator / front deskafter insurance posts; monthly statement cycles

Once insurance has paid, the remainder (deductible, coinsurance, anything over the annual max) is the patient’s. Practices often send confusing paper statements weeks later, patients don’t understand why they owe, and balances go stale. The agent generates a plain-language statement as soon as insurance posts, sends it by text/email with a pay link, answers simple “why do I owe this?” questions, and holds bills that shouldn’t go out yet (e.g. an appeal is still open).

Rule of thumb
bill only after insurance has finalized. Billing too early erodes trust
Tracking
sent → opened → paid / reminder 1 / reminder 2 → collections policy
